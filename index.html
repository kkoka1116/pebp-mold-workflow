Casting a pre-epiglottic baton plate in a 3D-printed mold

The pre-epiglottic baton plate, also called the Tübingen palatal plate, treats upper-airway obstruction in infants with Robin sequence. A curved extension, the baton, reaches back from a palatal plate to just above the epiglottis and holds the base of the tongue forward. This page shows how to make that appliance from a palate scan using printed tooling. The printer makes the mold. The worn appliance stays in the material the Tübingen group uses, cold-cure PMMA with a cast-in steel safety wire.

Status. Checked in software on five test cases. Nothing has been printed or cast, and no clinical team has reviewed this workflow. Resin choice, fill technique and print settings all need bench tests before any appliance goes near a patient. This is not a medical device.

The mold, piece by piece

Why molds instead of printing the appliance

Biocompatible PolyJet resins are rated for limited mucosal contact, under 24 hours. That is fine for the three prototypes tried in during awake nasopharyngoscopy, which are in the mouth for minutes. It is not enough for an appliance worn around the clock for weeks.

The Tübingen appliance is cold-cure PMMA (Orthocryl). So the printer's job becomes making precise tooling, and the appliance itself stays in a material with a long clinical track record.

Why four pieces

The baton leaves the plate heading backwards and then curves down toward the epiglottis. A one-piece rigid mold cannot be pulled off a curve like that. So the counter-mold is split three ways, and each piece comes away along one straight line.

  1. 1. The left baton half slides off to the left.
  2. 2. The right baton half slides off to the right.
  3. 3. The oral counter lifts straight up.
  4. 4. The appliance lifts off the working model.

The baton's cross-section is flat on its broad faces and rounded on its edges, so two halves split down its middle can slide apart sideways. For a baton imported from a file, the software fits the plane the baton actually lies in and splits on that plane. The published Tübingen cleft baton sits 2.6° off the left-right axis once placed, and a split on the plain axis left a thin undercut along its whole length.

Workflow

  1. 1

    Scan and design

    Scan the maxilla. The design software finds the landmarks, blocks out the cleft, builds the plate and writes three prototypes 2 mm apart sagittally.

  2. 2

    Prototypes for the endoscopy fitting

    Print prototypes 1 to 3 for the awake nasopharyngoscopy try-in. The software can export a multi-material version for a Stratasys printer using the MED-series biocompatible resins. A biocompatible dental resin on an SLA printer is the alternative. Either way, the clinical team decides which material it accepts for a few minutes of mucosal contact.

  3. 3

    Lock the design

    Record the endoscopy result, for example a shorter baton or a 2 mm anterior shift, and mark it final. The tooling is built from the last recorded adjustment. If there is none, it uses prototype 2.

  4. 4

    Generate and check the tooling

    The software writes every part of the kit and runs the checks below. It reports success only if every check passes. Step through the demold sequence in the viewer before printing.

  5. 5

    Print

    • Orientation. Keep supports off every molding surface. Print the working model base-down. Print the oral counter upside down, flat top on the plate and cavity up. Print each baton half on its outer side face, so the split face with the cavity and pins faces up.
    • Layers. Use 50 µm or finer for the model and counters.
    • Post-processing. Wash and post-cure fully to the resin maker's instructions. Under-cured resin is more likely to interact with methacrylate monomer.
    • Inspect. Under magnification, check the six wire pins: 2 mm diameter, with a slot that fits the 0.8 mm wire. Dry-fit all four pieces in the cradle.
  6. 6

    Prepare the mold

    1. Separator. Coat every molding surface with a separating medium suited to acrylic on printed models, unless the part is printed in a separation-model resin sold for use without one. Uncoated spots can bond.
    2. Safety wire. Cut the 1.8 × 0.8 mm twisted stainless wire to length, about 25 mm here. Bend it flatwise on the jig until it lies in the groove along its whole length.
    3. Bow wires. Bend the retention end of each 1.1 mm Remaloy bow wire so it sits in the middle of the plate thickness, inside the cavity.
    4. Seat the wire. Lay the left half split-face up and press the safety wire into the slots of its three pins. Close the right half over it.
    5. Close the mold. Set the oral counter on the working model and the baton halves behind it. Drop the bow wires into their grooves on the parting line. Set everything into the cradle.
    6. Seal. Close the outer ends of the bow grooves with wax. Hold the oral counter down with a clamp or an elastic band.
  7. 7

    Fill

    Tübingen builds the final plate with Orthocryl by the sprinkle (salt-and-pepper) technique on an open model. A closed mold needs a resin that flows: either a pour-type cold-cure PMMA, or the lab's usual resin mixed thinner. This is the main open decision and needs a bench test for porosity and working time.

    Fill a syringe and inject slowly through the sprue at the baton tip until resin appears at all three vents and both bow grooves. Tap the cradle to release bubbles. The rim of the plate is the high ground when poured, which is why the vents sit there.

  8. 8

    Cure

    Put the whole cradle into the pressure pot. Tübingen cures at 2.2 bar and 40 °C for at least 20 minutes in a Polymax 5. Curing under pressure reduces porosity and polymerization shrinkage.

  9. 9

    Demold

    Follow the order the software verified: left half off to the left, right half off to the right, oral counter straight up, then lift the appliance off the model.

  10. 10

    Finish

    1. Cut the sprue and the vent rods flush.
    2. Fill the six pin holes on the sides of the baton with fresh resin and pressure-cure again. The wire must not be exposed anywhere.
    3. Trim, round and polish the rim. Bend the bows to shape.
  11. 11

    Quality check and release

    • Plate thickness. Calipers should read at least 2.5 mm. Check the buccal rim separately: it was trimmed to vertical walls, which thins the last few millimetres.
    • Baton. Thickness at least 3 mm, and its width never reduced by more than half (Tübingen QC).
    • Wire. Not exposed anywhere, including at the filled pin holes.
    • Fit. Seat the appliance on the printed working model, and next to a printed copy of the designed appliance.
    • Release. Clean and disinfect according to the institution's protocol.

What the software checks

Every run checks that each part is a single watertight body, that the mold pieces do not overlap each other or intrude into the appliance, that the cavity is one connected space holding the whole appliance, and that the wire lies inside the appliance and clear of the pin slots.

The demolding test slides each piece along its pull direction in steps from 0.1 to 40 mm, past everything still in the flask. The overlap must stay within a tolerance of 2 mm³. The residual overlaps it absorbs are films a few microns thick where two surfaces touch, which is numerical noise rather than interference. These checks cover geometry only. They say nothing about how a real resin flows, shrinks or releases.

Where the cast appliance differs from the digital design

Vertical rim. The plate is the palate surface offset by its thickness. On the buccal slope of the ridge that offset leans outward by up to 1.9 mm, which would lock the oral counter. The software trims the rim to vertical walls along the drawn outline, the way a technician trims a plate. This removed 10.6 to 11.6% of the plate volume in the test cases, all from the outer buccal edge, and the footprint on the tissue is unchanged.

Pin holes. The pins leave six 2 mm holes in the sides of the baton, which are filled after demolding. Tübingen's own method also embeds the wire in a groove and covers it with fresh resin, so a post-fill is not new, but the bond should be checked on test pieces.

One colour. A single pour gives one colour. Tübingen colour-codes the extension dark blue and the base by plate number. Two-colour casting is possible but not designed yet.

Open questions for a clinical lab

  1. Which cold-cure resin and fill technique to use: pour-type resin by syringe, or the current resin mixed thinner.
  2. Which printed resin and separator pairing releases cleanly, tested on a scrap model and counter before a real case.
  3. Whether the notched pins are acceptable for the safety wire, or whether Tübingen's groove-and-cover method on a pin-free cast is preferred.
  4. Whether colour coding matters enough to design a two-stage pour.
  5. Who prints the tooling, and what quality and regulatory framework applies to custom appliances made this way.

Alternative: a flexible counter, like Tübingen's putty index

Tübingen transfers the chosen prototype to the final plate with an A-silicone putty index of 86 Shore A. The closest printed equivalent is the whole counter as one piece, printed in a flexible resin such as Formlabs Silicone 40A, or printed rigid and used as the master for a silicone index.

A flexible counter peels off the curved baton without being split. It cannot hold the wire pins, so it pairs with Tübingen's groove-and-cover wire method. The software exports this variant but does not check it, because the demolding test only applies to rigid pieces.
